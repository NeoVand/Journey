# Two different questions
THEO: For ages I thought the sandbox was the thing that pops up and asks me before a command runs.
NORA: [chuckles] You're far from alone, and it's the mix-up most worth clearing up. There are two questions here, not one. Before a call runs, the harness asks whether it may. Once it's running, something else decides what it can reach.
THEO: Two questions. Give them names.
NORA: The first is permission. The second is containment. Say the model asks to run pytest on the scheduler tests. Permission is your rules looking at that request and saying yes, no, or ask the human.
THEO: And containment?
NORA: The sandbox the command then runs in. It's a fence the operating system puts around the process, so it can only touch the files and the network it's been allowed. Think of it as glass around the commands the agent runs.
THEO: So it's like sudo. I say yes, and then it's allowed to do more.
NORA: And that's where it breaks. With sudo, saying yes widens what the command can touch. Here it doesn't. Where the glass is on, an approved command still runs inside it, with the same walls as before. And the walls only go around commands: the harness's own file edits and its other tools answer to the rules instead.
THEO: [skeptical] Why two systems, though? One good one should be enough.
NORA: Because they fail in different ways. A rule can be too loose. You can click yes too fast. The model can be fooled by something it read on a web page. The fence doesn't care what anyone believed: when the process reaches for something outside, the operating system says no.
THEO: Do all three agents draw the line in the same place?
NORA: Same split, different names. Claude Code's permission is a mode plus allow, ask and deny rules, and its glass is Seatbelt on macOS and bubblewrap on Linux and WSL2. Codex has an approval policy, which says when to ask, and a sandbox mode: read-only, workspace-write or full access.
THEO: And Copilot in VS Code?
NORA: Permission is confirmation cards and auto-approve lists. And its terminal sandbox is off unless you turn it on.
THEO: [surprised] Off. So there, once a command's approved, nothing fences it in.
NORA: Not until you switch it on. That's the classic surprise: people answer one question and assume they've answered both.
THEO: [thoughtful] One decides whether it may run. The other decides how far it gets.
NORA: Keep those apart, and you avoid the usual mistake: believing one layer, most often the sandbox, covers everything.
