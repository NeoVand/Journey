# It starts as a sentence
NORA: Forty-four characters and a press of Return. "Fix the race condition in the job scheduler." For now, that's the whole of it.
THEO: And that's already on its way to the model?
NORA: Not even close. Right now it's keystrokes, held by a program on your laptop. In Claude Code or Codex you typed it into a terminal. In Copilot, into the chat panel in VS Code. The model hasn't seen a thing.
THEO: [skeptical] It doesn't feel that way. I press Return and something starts moving straight away.
NORA: Something does. The program you typed into wakes up. That's the harness, the part you actually installed. The model lives somewhere else entirely, in a data center, and on your side, the harness is the only thing that ever talks to it.
THEO: So the agent on my laptop is a sort of receptionist, and the real brain is upstairs.
NORA: [amused] Close, but a receptionist only passes messages along. This one does all the work with its hands. It reads your files, runs your tests, asks you for permission. The brain upstairs only reads text and writes text. It never touches your machine.
THEO: Okay. The card also says nothing here is a token yet. I thought everything in AI was tokens.
NORA: Eventually. A token is a small piece of text, usually a word or part of a word. "Scheduler" might be one token, "race condition" two. But tokens are made by the provider, the company running the model, at its front door. On your laptop it's plain text the whole way.
THEO: [curious] My agent shows me a token count, though. Where does that come from?
NORA: The harness may estimate one for itself, say, to decide when the conversation is getting too long. But the real count comes back from the provider's server with every reply. The harness never cuts your sentence up and sends the pieces. It sends text.
THEO: Fine. And the card says my request will take twelve forms on this trip.
NORA: Twelve, by the book's count, and the counter in the corner ticks them off. Keystrokes are number one. Before it leaves your laptop it'll be a page of structured text. On the internet, scrambled bytes. By the time the model reads it, a row of numbers.
THEO: So my sentence literally turns into the fix at the end?
NORA: Not literally, and the book is careful about that. A character, a token, a network packet and a file write are different units, and they don't map one to one. Your words shape what the model writes. They don't become it. The twelve forms follow the task, not one particular token.
THEO: [thoughtful] Does any of this start differently depending on which agent I use?
NORA: The first step is the same. Where the loop runs differs a little. Claude Code is one program, and the terminal, the VS Code extension and the desktop app all run that same program. Codex's terminal app runs the agent loop in its own process, and its VS Code extension and desktop app reach the same engine through something called codex app-server.
THEO: And Copilot?
NORA: Copilot is layered. VS Code owns the editor, the extension gathers context, and since mid-2026 the loop itself runs in a separate process called the Agent Host.
THEO: Three shapes underneath, and in all of them, my sentence is just sitting there as text.
NORA: Just text, on your machine, untouched. Then the harness starts packing.
