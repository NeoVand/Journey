# The transformer
NORA: Picture a stack of maps, floating one above another, eighty high. Every word of your prompt lands on the bottom one, and then it climbs.
THEO: [curious] That's the model? I was expecting a wiring diagram.
NORA: There's a small one on the card, and it's accurate. But the maps are the better way in. Every one of these chips, whether it's reading prompts or writing answers, runs the same kind of program. It's called a transformer. It's the T in GPT, and it's the design behind today's big language models.
THEO: And what does it actually do?
NORA: In one line: tokens go in at the bottom, and a score for every possible next word comes out at the top. Everything else is how it gets from one to the other.
THEO: Tokens being the pieces my prompt got cut into.
NORA: Right, and by now each one is just a number. "Fix the race condition in the job scheduler" is a short row of them at the very end of a much longer request: the agent's instructions, the tool descriptions, your project's rules. In this example, about twenty-one thousand tokens on the first turn.
THEO: So walk me up the stack.
NORA: At the bottom, each token's number becomes a long list of numbers, which you can read as a place on a map of meaning. Then come the layers, and each one does two things. First, every token looks back at the tokens before it and takes in what it needs. That's called attention. Then each token goes through a small network on its own.
THEO: And then the next layer.
NORA: Same design, its own learned numbers, starting where the last one finished. Eighty is an example number. And because the site draws every layer as the same map, you can watch the words move. "Job" starts out among careers, and as it climbs, it drifts toward code.
THEO: [skeptical] Words sliding around a map. Isn't that a bit of a cartoon?
NORA: [laughs] A little, and the site says so. The real map has thousands of dimensions, not two, and the neighborhoods are illustrative. But the motion is the honest part. Each layer changes what the model takes a word to mean in this sentence, and on a map, a change of meaning is a move.
THEO: And at the top?
NORA: Only the last position matters. A final step called the LM head turns it into one raw score for every entry in the vocabulary, a hundred thousand to two hundred thousand of them. Strictly, those entries are tokens, not words. "Word" is the friendly version.
THEO: [surprised] So what comes out is scores. Not text?
NORA: Not text, not edits, not commands. Scores. Picking one token from them is a separate step, and it's the only roll of the dice in the whole journey.
THEO: Here's my sharp one. Does it learn from me while it does this? Does my prompt change the model?
NORA: No. Those learned numbers are called weights. Training set them, and using the model never changes them. Your prompt flows up through the floors and leaves them exactly as they were.
THEO: [thoughtful] Is it the same for every agent? I use Claude Code, my colleague's on Copilot.
NORA: From here on, all three look the same: Claude Code, Codex, Copilot in VS Code. One caution, though. Providers don't publish exactly how their models are built. What you're seeing is the public blueprint, drawn from open engines and papers.
THEO: So: numbers in at the bottom, floor after floor of looking back and then working alone, and a score for every token out of the top.
NORA: [warmly] And no hands anywhere. Just one very large calculation, run again for every token it writes. Let's start at the bottom.
